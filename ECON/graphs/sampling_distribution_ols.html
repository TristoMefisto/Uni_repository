The sampling distribution of the OLS slope

The slides state the two properties as theorems: under SLR.1-SLR.4 OLS is unbiased, and under SLR.5 its variance is σ²/SSTx. Here they are watched instead of proved. The population is simulated and built to look like WAGE1: wage = −0.90 + 0.54·educ + u, education with mean 12.6 and SD 2.8, and an error SD of 3.4. Every press draws new samples of n workers from it and runs OLS on each.

  1. Draw 1,000 samples with all the assumptions on. The histogram of β̂1 is centred on the true 0.54, and its spread matches the dashed curve from σ²/SSTx.
  2. Change n, σ and the spread of education one at a time and see which way the histogram narrows or widens, as the variance formula says.
  3. Break SLR.4 (correlate u with x): the histogram moves off 0.54. Break SLR.5 instead: it stays centred, but the usual formula gets the spread wrong.

Unbiasedness is a statement about the histogram, not about one sample: any single β̂1 can be far from 0.54, but the estimates average out to it. The proof uses only E(u|x) = 0, which is why making u correlated with x (as when ability sits in u and goes with education) moves the whole histogram. That bias does not shrink with n: more data give a tighter histogram around the wrong number.

The variance formula has three ingredients and each slider moves one: a larger σ² spreads the estimates, and more data or more spread in x (a larger SSTx = (n − 1)·Var(x)) tighten them. Heteroskedasticity leaves the centre alone (MLR.5 is not used to prove unbiasedness) but makes σ²/SSTx the wrong variance. When the error variance grows with x, the formula understates the true spread, so the usual standard errors are too small. That is the problem robust standard errors solve (the heteroskedasticity graph of lecture 06).

Dividing SSR by n − 2 rather than n accounts for the two first-order conditions (Σû = 0, Σxû = 0) that use up 2 degrees of freedom. With n = 15 the difference is visible, with n = 500 it is not.
