Fitting the OLS line

OLS picks the intercept and slope that make the sum of squared residuals as small as possible. Here you pick them yourself, on the textbook data the slides' examples come from: WAGE1 (526 workers, 1976) for wage on education, in levels and in logs, and CEOSAL1 (209 CEOs) for the log-log salary-sales elasticity of slides 106-108. The last tab is eight points you can drag.

  1. Move the slope (the line turns around x̄) and its height, and watch the sum of squared residuals in the second panel. It is smallest exactly at the OLS line (dashed).
  2. Look at the third panel: SST = SSE + SSR holds only at the OLS line. Anywhere else a cross term appears, and the residuals no longer sum to zero.
  3. Change the units (wage in cents, education in months) and see which numbers move and which (R², t) do not.

The two first-order conditions of OLS are Σû = 0 and Σxû = 0. They are why the OLS line goes through (x̄, ȳ), why the residuals average to zero, and why the cross term in the decomposition vanishes, so SST = SSE + SSR is a property of the OLS line, not of any line. Moving your line away from OLS always raises SSR, and the curve in the second panel is a parabola because SSR is quadratic in b1.

The slides' own wage example uses a different WAGE2 file (759 men in 1991), not distributed with the textbook, where wage = −5.12 + 1.43 educ (R² = .133) and log(wage) = 1.142 + .099 educ (R² = .165). The textbook's WAGE1 used here gives the same story with smaller numbers: about 54 cents an hour, or 8.3%, per year of education. The CEOSAL1 elasticity reproduces the slides exactly: .257, so 10% more sales go with about 2.57% more salary.

Units only rescale. Measuring wage in cents multiplies both coefficients and their standard errors by 100. Measuring education in months divides the slope by 12. R² and the t statistic do not move. In a log model, rescaling y only shifts the intercept by log(c), which is why the elasticity is free of units.
