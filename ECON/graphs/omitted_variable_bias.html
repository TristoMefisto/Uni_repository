Omitted variable bias

Leaving x₂ out of the regression changes the coefficient on x₁ by exactly β̂₂·δ̃₁, where δ̃₁ is the slope from regressing x₂ on x₁. The first tab takes the identity apart on the slides' two cases (slides 182-184). The second simulates it, to show what the identity means for the bias and the variance of the short regression as the sample grows.

  2. Raise n: both histograms tighten, but the red one tightens around the wrong number. The bias does not shrink with n.
  3. The second panel shows the trade-off as RMSE against n: the biased short regression wins only in small samples.

The identity holds in every sample, whatever the truth: it is algebra of OLS, not an assumption. It becomes a statement about bias once we take expectations, and then only β₂ and the correlation matter. In the education and IQ case both are positive, so the simple regression overstates the return to education (9.9% against 7.3%). In the attendance case prior GPA raises the score but goes with fewer missed lectures, so the short regression makes missing look harmful (−.121) when, holding prior GPA fixed, it is not (+.017).

The simulation adds the slides' second point (214-217). The short regression has the smaller variance whenever x₁ and x₂ are correlated, so with β₂ ≠ 0 there is a real trade-off. It is a bad one in practice: the variance falls with n and the bias does not, and the slide's variance comparison holds σ² fixed, while leaving out a relevant x₂ actually raises the error variance (the simulation lets it). With β₂ = 0 there is no trade-off at all: the short regression is unbiased and more precise, so adding an irrelevant but correlated variable only costs precision.
