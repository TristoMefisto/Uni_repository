Dummy variables and interactions

A dummy shifts the intercept; a dummy times a continuous variable shifts the slope. Both tabs run on Wooldridge's WAGE1 (526 workers, 1976) with log(wage) as the dependent variable, so coefficients read as approximate percentage differences. The second tab reproduces the four-group example of slide 429 exactly. The slides' own intercept-and-slope example uses a COLLEGE.DTA extract (n = 750) that is not distributed with the textbook; its published lines are available as a separate option.

  1. Start with "female only": the coefficient is the gap between the two group means, nothing else.
  2. Add a regressor: the two lines become parallel, and the gap is now measured at the same education (or experience).
  3. Add the interaction: the lines may diverge or converge, so the gap depends on where you measure it. Move the slider to read it.
  1. Four mutually exclusive groups from female and married. With single men as the base the coefficients are .213, −.198 and −.110, as on slide 429.
  2. Change the base group: every coefficient changes, because each is now relative to a different group. The bars just move the zero line.
  3. The fitted wage of any worker does not change at all. Tick "all four dummies" to see why one group must be left out.

The dummy variable trap

With female alone, OLS returns the difference between the average log wage of women and of men: about −.40, women earning about 33% less. Holding experience fixed changes the number because men and women differ in experience; it measures the gap between a man and a woman with the same experience. The interaction lets that gap depend on experience itself. In WAGE1 the gap widens with experience; in the slides' COLLEGE extract it narrows (women's slope .0517 against men's .0283). Whether δ₁ = 0 is a t test, and whether women and men have the same line altogether (δ₀ = δ₁ = 0) is an F test.

With g categories we include g − 1 dummies: the omitted one is the base group, and every dummy coefficient is a difference from it. Including all g together with the intercept breaks MLR.3, because the dummies add up to the constant. The choice of base is a matter of convenience: it decides which differences come with a standard error printed next to them (here married women against single women, −.088 with t = −1.68, needs either lincom or a change of base).
