Heteroskedasticity and robust standard errors

When the variance of u changes with x, OLS stays unbiased and consistent but the usual standard errors are wrong, and so are the t tests and confidence intervals built on them. The first tab simulates it: a population with a known slope of 0.5 and an error SD that grows with x, so we can count how often each kind of standard error rejects a null that is true. The second tab compares the two standard errors on Wooldridge's WAGE1 (526 workers).

  1. Set the heteroskedasticity to 0 and draw: both standard errors reject the true H₀: β₁ = 0.5 about 5% of the time, as a 5% test should.
  2. Raise it. The fan opens in the first panel; the usual t statistics spread out beyond N(0, 1) and reject too often, the robust ones stay on target.
  3. Press Sweep to see the two rejection rates against the strength of the heteroskedasticity, and lower n to see the robust test misbehave in small samples.
  1. Pick the dependent variable. In levels the residuals fan out with education: high earners vary more.
  2. The table gives both standard errors for every coefficient. In levels the robust ones are clearly larger for educ, in logs the two are close.
  3. The coefficients never change: robust inference changes only how uncertain we say they are.

Heteroskedasticity breaks only the variance formula. The robust estimate replaces the unknown σᵢ² with each observation's squared residual, which is noisy for one observation but averages out over many, so it is consistent whatever the form of the heteroskedasticity, and it is also right when there is none. The price is that it is justified only in large samples: at n = 20 it is too small on average and the robust test over-rejects even under homoskedasticity (slide 379), which is the one argument for the usual standard errors.

In the slides' COLLEGE.DTA example (slides 381-383) the robust standard errors are slightly larger than the usual ones (.0325 against .0318 for female) and nothing changes. In WAGE1 with wage in levels the gap is bigger, and taking logs mostly removes it: the log transformation often brings the data closer to MLR.5 (slide 390).
