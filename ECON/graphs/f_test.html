Joint hypotheses: the F test and the adjusted R²

Wooldridge's MLB1 data on major league baseball salaries, the example of slides 311-325. Do batting average, home runs and runs batted in matter for salary once experience and games played are held fixed? None of the three is significant on its own, yet together they are. Every number here comes from regressions run live on the 353 players. The slides' copy of the file has 330 players and gives F = 8.34; the textbook's 353 give 9.55.

When it rises (slide 409)

The F test compares two fits. Imposing H₀ can only make the fit worse, so F ≥ 0, and it is large when the restricted model loses much more than chance would explain. Here dropping the three performance measures raises the SSR from 183.19 to 198.31: F = ((198.31 − 183.19)/3)/(183.19/347) = 9.55, far beyond the 1% critical value of about 3.8. The individual t tests miss this because hrunsyr and rbisyr measure almost the same thing, so each one's separate contribution is imprecise; their joint contribution is not. The F test cannot say which of the three matters.

The adjusted R² is not a test but a fit measure with a penalty. Its rule (rise iff |t| > 1) shows how weak that penalty is compared with a 5% test, which needs |t| above about 1.97. It is useful for comparing models that are not nested; for nested ones the F test is the right tool.
