Maximum likelihood for logit and probit

Logit and probit are estimated by choosing the β that makes the observed sample most probable. The first tab is the slides' own toy example (slides 476-478): three observations, y = 1 at x = 1.5 and x = 0.6, y = 0 at x = 0.7. The second tab runs the same algorithm on the 753 women of MROZ and turns the log-likelihood into the pseudo R² and the likelihood ratio test.

  1. Drag the point in the first panel (or use the sliders): it is a candidate (β₀, β₁). The contours are the log-likelihood, highest in the middle.
  2. The second panel draws G(β₀ + β₁x) for that candidate and, for each observation, the probability it assigns to what was actually observed. The likelihood is their product.
  3. Press Climb: Newton-Raphson walks uphill to the maximum, the MLE. No closed form exists; the computer finds it numerically.
  1. The first panel is the log-likelihood at each iteration, starting from the intercept-only model (−514.87) and settling at −401.30 for probit, as Stata's log on slide 499.
  2. Untick variables to impose that their coefficients are 0. The restricted model has a lower log-likelihood; twice the drop is the LR statistic.
  3. The LR statistic is compared with a χ² with as many degrees of freedom as restrictions. Dropping all seven gives the overall LR χ²(7) = 227.14.

Maximum likelihood answers a question: which parameter values would make this sample most likely? With G the normal cdf it is probit, with the logistic cdf logit. There is no formula for the maximum, so software climbs the surface with Newton-Raphson, using its slope and curvature; that is what Stata's "Iteration 0, 1, 2…" log shows. In large samples the MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal and efficient (slide 481), and its standard errors come from the curvature at the top: a flat surface, like the one of three observations, means large standard errors.

The log-likelihood is always negative for binary data (it is a sum of logs of probabilities), and adding variables can only raise it. The pseudo R² measures how far the model moves log L from the intercept-only value, and the LR test asks whether a group of variables raises it by more than chance: it plays the role the F test plays after OLS.
