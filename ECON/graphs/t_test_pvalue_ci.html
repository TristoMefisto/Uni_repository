The t test, the p-value and the confidence interval

One estimate, one standard error and the degrees of freedom are all a t test needs. The presets are the slides' own Stata output (slides 255-286), so every number on the page can be checked against them. The three panels are the same test seen three ways: on the t scale, on the scale of the coefficient, and through the critical value.

  1. Pick a preset. The first panel shades the rejection region in red and the p-value in orange: we reject exactly when the orange area is smaller than α.
  2. Switch the alternative between two-sided and one-sided and watch the rejection region move to one tail and the p-value halve.
  3. The second panel is the confidence interval: the null value lies outside it exactly when the two-sided test rejects at the same α.

The p-value is the smallest significance level at which the null would be rejected, so it carries more information than "reject at 5%". Two things change it besides the data: the null value (testing H₀: β = 1 for the crime elasticity gives t = 2.46, not the 11.57 Stata prints for H₀: β = 0) and the alternative (a one-sided p-value is half the two-sided one when the estimate is on the side of H₁, and above 0.5 when it is on the wrong side).

The interval and the two-sided test are one object: the 95% interval collects all the null values a 5% two-sided test would not reject. The degrees of freedom matter only in small samples: with df = 18 the 5% one-sided critical value is 1.734 (slide 258), against 1.645 for the normal.
