Consistency and asymptotic normality

The number of arrests in 1986 in Wooldridge's CRIME1 (2,725 young men, slide 357) is a count with 72% zeros: nothing like the normal distribution MLR.6 asks for. This page treats the 2,725 men as the population, draws samples of n from it, and regresses arrests on quarters employed in 1986 (qemp86) in each. The population slope is −.0929. As n grows the estimates pile up on it (consistency) and their standardised version turns normal (asymptotic normality), so t tests work without MLR.6 (with one caveat about MLR.5 that the page also shows).

  1. Draw 1,000 samples at n = 10, then at n = 100 and n = 1,000. The histogram of β̂₁ narrows around −.0929 and SD·√n stays about the same: the spread falls like 1/√n.
  2. Look at the t statistics: at n = 10 their histogram is lopsided and the 5% test rejects a true null 12% of the time. With the usual standard errors it never quite gets to 5%, because MLR.5 fails here; switch to robust ones and it does.
  3. Switch the target to the effect holding income fixed (−.0421). The simple regression still converges, but to −.0929: omitting income makes it inconsistent, and no n fixes that.

Consistency is about where the estimator ends up, not about any one sample: the sampling distribution collapses onto β₁ as n → ∞. It needs only zero correlation between x and u (MLR.4′), weaker than the zero conditional mean used for unbiasedness. Asymptotic normality is what lets us keep using t and F tests with a dependent variable like this one: the CLT works on the averages inside β̂₁, whatever the shape of u, so the t statistic is approximately N(0, 1) in large samples. How large is large depends on the data: with a skewed count like narr86 n = 10 is not enough, n = 100 nearly is. The theorem of slide 358 assumes MLR.1-MLR.5, though. Arrests are more variable among men who worked fewer quarters, so the usual standard error is slightly too small at any n and the 5% test rejects about 7% of the time even with 1,000 observations. Heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors (lecture 06) fix it in large samples (about 5% at n = 1,000 and above), but at n = 10 they are worse than the usual ones, rejecting about 20% of the time: they too are only justified asymptotically (slide 379).

Omitting a relevant variable is a different kind of failure. Here legal income in 1986 (inc86) lowers arrests and is strongly correlated with quarters employed (δ₁ = 29.45), so the simple regression converges to −.0421 + (−.00173)(29.45) = −.0929. More data only make us more confident about the wrong number, which is why the false rejection rate climbs towards 100% with n.
