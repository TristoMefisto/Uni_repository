Correlation, causation and ceteris paribus

Slide 16's table: 9% of the 400 boy scouts were delinquent against 15% of the 400 non-scouts. Does scouting prevent delinquency? Not necessarily: a third variable Z (say family background, or how often the family goes to church) can drive both scouting and delinquency. This page splits the 800 boys by a three-level Z. The default split is constructed so that it adds up exactly to the slide's table while scouting has no effect at all within any level of Z. The sliders then let you change how strongly Z drives each variable, and add a true effect.

  1. Start from the slide's table: scouts are 6 points less delinquent overall (first panel).
  2. Look inside each level of Z (second panel): scouts and non-scouts have exactly the same rate. The overall gap comes from scouts being concentrated where delinquency is low anyway.
  3. Move the sliders. With no confounding the overall gap equals the true effect; with confounding the two separate. Holding Z fixed is what "ceteris paribus" means, and what adding Z to a regression does.

Slide 14's three criteria for a causal claim are correlation, time order and the exclusion of alternative explanations. The table passes the first and fails the third as long as a variable like Z is not accounted for: comparing all scouts with all non-scouts compares boys who differ in Z as well. Comparing scouts and non-scouts with the same Z removes that alternative, and is exactly what a multiple regression with Z among the regressors does. The difference between the simple and the controlled comparison is the omitted variable bias of lecture 03.

Two things no table can fix. Z must be measured, and it must be the only confounder: an unmeasured one reopens the problem, which is why the slides call MLR.4 the crucial assumption. And the time order must be right: slide 17 points out that delinquent boys may avoid scouting, so the arrow could run from D to S. The split by Z here is constructed for the illustration; the slides give only the two-way table.
