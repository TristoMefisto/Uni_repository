Pooled OLS, first differences, fixed and random effects

Panel data let us remove a time-constant unobserved effect aᵢ that pooled OLS leaves in the error. The first tab simulates a panel where aᵢ is correlated with x (the true β₁ is 1), so the estimators visibly disagree, and shows that POLS, RE and FE are one regression with a different share of each unit's mean removed. The second tab is the slides' job-training example on JTRAIN (54 firms, 1987-1989, slides 535-585), estimated live with firm-clustered standard errors.

  1. Each colour is one unit observed T times. Within a unit the points rise with slope 1; across units, those with a larger aᵢ also have a larger x, so the pooled slope (λ = 0) is too steep.
  2. Press Animate: each unit is pulled towards its own mean. At λ = 1 only the within variation is left and the slope is the FE estimate. In between, at λ = θ̂, is RE.
  3. Draw 500 panels for the sampling distributions. Set Corr(aᵢ, x) = 0: then all four are centred on 1 and RE is the most precise.
  1. The first panel follows each firm's log scrap rate over the three years. Firms that got a grant in 1988 (orange) started with higher scrap rates than those that never did.
  2. So pooled OLS, which compares firms with each other, finds no effect or a positive one. FD and FE compare each firm with itself and find that the grant lowers scrap.

The four estimators differ in what variation they use. Pooled OLS uses all of it, including the differences between units, which carry aᵢ: if aᵢ is correlated with x it is inconsistent, here by ρσₐ. FD and FE use only the changes within each unit, so aᵢ drops out and they are consistent under strict exogeneity, whatever the correlation between aᵢ and x. RE removes only a fraction θ of each unit's mean: it keeps some between variation, which makes it more precise but also inherits the POLS bias whenever Cov(aᵢ, x) ≠ 0. As θ̂ → 1 (large T, or σₐ² large relative to σᵤ²) RE becomes FE; as θ̂ → 0 it becomes POLS (slide 601).

In JTRAIN the pooled estimates have the wrong sign because grants went to firms with higher scrap rates to begin with (the firm effect). Comparing each firm with itself, a grant lowers scrap by about 25% in the year it is received and by about 42% the year after (FE, slide 585), although with 54 firms the intervals are wide.
