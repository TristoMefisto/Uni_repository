Partialling out (Frisch-Waugh-Lovell)

The slides' ATTEND example (680 students): the final exam score regressed on lectures missed gives −.121, but once prior GPA is added the coefficient on missed becomes +.017. The Frisch-Waugh-Lovell theorem says why: the multiple regression coefficient is the simple regression of y on the part of x1 that x2 cannot explain. This page removes that part step by step, on the real data.

  1. Start at λ = 0: the first panel is the simple regression of final on missed, slope −.121. The colours are prior GPA: good students (orange) miss few lectures and score high.
  2. Press Animate. Each student slides sideways as the part of missed predicted by prior GPA is removed. At λ = 1 the slope is +.017, the multiple regression coefficient.
  3. The third panel nets prior GPA out of the score as well (the added-variable plot): same slope, and the residuals are the multiple regression's residuals.

The simple regression attributes to missed lectures what belongs to prior GPA: students with a high prior GPA miss fewer lectures (Corr = −.427) and would score higher anyway, so missing looks costly. Holding prior GPA fixed, which is what the multiple regression does, the effect is about zero (and not significant: t = 0.50). FWL makes "holding fixed" literal: the coefficient comes only from the variation in missed that is left once prior GPA has been used to predict it.

The same residual explains the variance formula of lecture 03: Var(β̂ⱼ) = σ²/(SSTⱼ(1 − Rⱼ²)) has Σr̂²ij in the denominator. The more x₂ predicts x₁ (a high R₁²), the less variation is left in r̂₁ and the less precise β̂₁ is. The intermediate values of λ are only a way to watch the slide: no regression produces them, and the slope reaches β̂₁ only at λ = 1.
