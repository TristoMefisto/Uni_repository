Precision of OLS and the Gauss-Markov theorem

Under MLR.1-MLR.5 the variance of each slope is σ²/(SSTj(1 − Rj²)). The first tab moves its three ingredients one at a time. The second tab is the Gauss-Markov theorem: among all estimators that are linear in y and unbiased, OLS has the smallest variance, and it loses that title when the errors are heteroskedastic. Both tabs are simulations: the numbers are chosen to show the mechanism, not taken from a data set.

OLS weights and the theorem

Collinearity is a problem of precision, not of bias, and the variance formula says it can be offset by the other two ingredients: VIF times more data gives back the same SD. It also explains slide 341's remark on policy evaluation: controls that are highly correlated with each other inflate their own standard errors, not the one of the treatment variable, as long as the treatment is not itself well explained by them.

The Gauss-Markov theorem compares OLS only with estimators that are linear in y and unbiased. The grouping and extreme-point estimators qualify (their weights depend only on x and satisfy the two conditions), and both lose to OLS because they throw away information: the middle observations, or all but two. When MLR.5 fails, the theorem fails with it: weighting each observation by 1/σᵢ² gives a smaller variance than OLS. In practice the σᵢ² are unknown, which is why lecture 06 keeps OLS and fixes the standard errors instead.
