A single-layer neural network, trained live

The Wage data of the R lab: the wage (in thousands of dollars) of 3,000 male workers against their age, the same data as the splines page. A neural network with one hidden layer of K units learns the curve in the browser by stochastic gradient descent. 1,000 workers are kept aside as a validation set (for early stopping) and 1,000 as a test set; the network is trained on n of the remaining ones. Every number on the page comes from the network as it is right now, so the page changes while it trains.

  1. Press Train. Watch the hidden units (top right) move their kinks and slopes, and the fit (top left), which is their weighted sum, bend into the shape of the data.
  2. Press "Overfit": 30 workers and 20 units, trained for long. The training error keeps falling while the validation error turns up. Then try early stopping, or a ridge penalty.
  3. Move the mouse over the fit to see which units fire at each age (the network, bottom left), and click a unit to switch it off.

Formulas, with the current numbers

The network
one predictor (age), K hidden units, a linear output layer
The activation (ReLU)
what each hidden unit does to its linear combination of the input
The objective
squared error averaged over the n training workers, plus a ridge penalty on the weights (not the intercepts)
Gradient descent
each step moves all the parameters a little against the slope of R, computed on a minibatch of 32 workers

What the network is. Each hidden unit takes a linear combination of the input, wk0 + wk1 age, and passes it through g. With ReLU this gives a hinge: flat at 0 on one side of the age −wk0/wk1, a straight line on the other. The output layer is a linear regression on the K activations, so the fit is a sum of K hinges plus a constant, which is a piecewise-linear curve with (at most) K kinks. That is a linear spline, the same family as on the splines page, with one difference: there the knots were fixed before fitting, here they are parameters that gradient descent moves around. With the sigmoid each unit is a smooth step instead, and the fit is a sum of K smooth steps. Without a nonlinear g the units would add up to a straight line, whatever K is.

How it is trained. Age and wage are standardised inside the fit (every number shown is back in years and dollars), and the starting weights put each unit's kink at a random age inside the data. An epoch shuffles the training workers, cuts them into minibatches of 32 and takes one gradient step per minibatch, so with n = 1,000 an epoch is 32 steps and with n = 30 it is one step. This is the stochastic gradient descent of the slides: each step uses the slope on a minibatch, not on the whole training set, which is cheaper and noisier. The objective is not convex, so different seeds (different starting weights) end in different fits with similar errors.

Overfitting, early stopping, ridge. With 400 or 1,000 workers and a few units the network cannot overfit much: the three errors move together. With 30 workers and 20 units it has 61 parameters for 30 points, and the late epochs fit the noise of those 30 wages: the training error keeps falling while the validation error rises. Two fixes from the slides. Slow learning with early stopping keeps the weights of the epoch with the lowest validation error (the button jumps back to them), and the test error then says how well that choice does on workers used for nothing else. A ridge penalty shrinks the weights towards 0 and keeps the units from bending sharply around single points. The wage data are noisy (the MSE of the best fits stays around 1,600, a typical error of about 40 thousand dollars), so the validation and test errors also differ a little between seeds just because each set gets a different share of the very high earners.
