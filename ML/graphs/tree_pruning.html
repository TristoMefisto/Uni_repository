Growing and pruning a regression tree

The Hitters data from the slides: the log salary of 263 baseball players, predicted from the years they have played in the major leagues and the hits they made in the previous year. The page goes through Algorithm 8.1 one step at a time. First the tree is grown by recursive binary splitting, one split per step, until the stopping rules bite. Then it is pruned back with cost complexity pruning, one weakest link per step, until only the root is left. Every panel shows the same tree at the current step, and the box under the buttons says in words what just happened. The third tab does the same for a classification tree, on the Carseats data of the R lab: whether a store sells more than 8 thousand child car seats, predicted from its price and its local advertising budget.

  1. Press Next (or Play) from the start. Each step makes one split: watch the orange box in the predictor space, the new branch in the tree, the new cliff in the 3D surface.
  2. Keep going past the unpruned tree. Each step now removes the weakest link, and the bar chart shows why that one goes first.
  3. Drag the 3D surface to rotate it, and jump to the size cross-validation picks to compare the training, cross-validation and test errors.

Growing. Recursive binary splitting is top-down and greedy. At each step it looks at every box, every predictor and every cutpoint between two observed values, and makes the single split that lowers the RSS the most (the bottom-left panel shows that search for the box just split). It never looks ahead, so a cut that looks poor now is never made, even if it would open the way to a very good one later. Here the boxes are split in order of how much they gain (the "split one of the regions" of the slides). Splitting them depth-first instead would give exactly the same final tree, only in a different order. The stopping rules are modelled on the defaults of R's tree(): a box is split only if it holds at least 10 players, each child keeps at least 5, and the split saves at least 1% of the root's RSS. On all 263 players the unpruned tree has 29 leaves.

Why prune. Every split lowers the training RSS, so the training MSE falls at every step of the growing phase. The test MSE does not. The first two splits do almost all the work, and the late ones fit the noise of the few players inside a small box. Stopping early (only split when the RSS drop is large) would be short-sighted, for the reason above, so the tree is grown large and then cut back.

Pruning. For a given α, cost complexity pruning keeps the subtree that minimises RSS + α|T|. Take an internal node t with |Tt| leaves under it. Collapsing it into one leaf raises the RSS by RSS(t) − RSS(Tt) and removes |Tt| − 1 leaves from the penalty, so it pays off once α is larger than the ratio in the last formula card. The node with the smallest ratio (the weakest link) is the first to go as α rises. After it collapses the ratios are recomputed and the next weakest goes, and so on up to the root. This is why the subtrees are nested, and why only about as many subtrees as leaves need checking, not every possible subtree.

Choosing α. Each of the 6 folds grows and prunes its own tree, and its subtree for each α is scored on the left-out players. Pruning the 263-player tree to 3 leaves gives exactly the tree of the slides: Years < 4.5 with a mean log salary of 5.107 (about 165 thousand dollars), then Hits < 117.5 with 5.998 and 6.740. The cross-validation curve is flat near its minimum, so the size it picks moves with the seed. The default seed lands on 3 leaves, as on the slides, other seeds anything from 3 to about 12. What stays the same is that a handful of leaves does as well as twenty.

The surface. A regression tree is a step function of the predictors: flat over each box, with a cliff at every cut. The 3D panel makes two things visible that the tree diagram hides. The Hits cliff exists only on the Years ≥ 4.5 side, which is the interaction the tree builds by itself (hits matter only for experienced players). And every cliff runs parallel to an axis, because each split uses one predictor at a time.

Classification tree. The third tab uses the Carseats data of the R lab: 400 stores, with High = Yes when unit sales are above 8 thousand (164 stores), predicted from Price and Advertising only (2 of the 10 predictors, so the boxes can be drawn). Half the stores train the tree and half test it, as in the lab. A leaf predicts its most common class, and the tree is grown with the Gini index (or the entropy) but pruned with the classification error rate, as prune.misclass does. The two criteria disagree on purpose. With the default seed, split 4 cuts the stores priced at 127.5 or more at Advertising 8.5: 10% of them have high sales on one side and 42% on the other, so both sides predict No and the error rate does not move, yet n·G falls from 21.97 to 18.89 because both halves are purer than the box was. Growing keeps it (a purer node can open the way to a useful split later, and its leaves are more certain), while pruning by the error rate sees no value in it and removes it first, at α = 0. The JS tree reproduces rpart on all 400 stores without the 1% rule: the same root split (Price < 92.5), and the same pruning sequence, from 57 leaves (86 stores misclassified) through 23, 19, 16, 10, 6, 4 and 2 leaves (130 misclassified) down to the root (164), at the same values of α.
