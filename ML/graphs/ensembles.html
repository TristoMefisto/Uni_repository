Bagging, random forests and boosting

The Boston data of the R lab: the median house value in 506 suburbs, predicted from 12 characteristics, with the same random half as training set (set.seed(1) in R). Every curve is a real ensemble of regression trees fit in the browser, so the bootstrap draws are random (the seed slider redraws them). The test error is measured on the other 253 suburbs. The three pills show the three methods one at a time.

  1. Press Grow and watch the test error fall far below the single tree as more bootstrapped trees are averaged, then flatten: a large B does not overfit.
  2. Compare the dashed out-of-bag curve with the test curve: it estimates the test error from the training suburbs alone.
  3. Look at the middle panel: almost every bagged tree splits on rm first, so the trees look alike.
  1. Start from m = 12 (which is bagging) and lower m: the trees stop all splitting on rm first (middle panel).
  2. Watch the green curve of the forest drop below the navy curve of bagging as the trees become less correlated.
  3. Try m = 6 (the R lab) and m = 4 (about p/3, the default for regression), and change the seed to see how much is noise.
  1. Press Grow: the training error goes towards zero while the test error settles, and only creeps up slowly.
  2. Raise λ to see boosting learn faster, lower it to see it need many more trees.
  3. Switch to stumps (d = 1): each tree uses one predictor, so the model is additive.

Formulas, with the current numbers

Bagging
the average of B trees grown on bootstrap samples
Out-of-bag share
the chance that a training observation is left out of one bootstrap sample of size n
Variance of a correlated average
B trees with variance σ² and pairwise correlation ρ
Random forest
share of splits that cannot use a given predictor
Boosting
each new tree is fit to the residuals and shrunk
The boosted model
the sum of all the shrunken trees

Bagging grows deep trees (until a node holds 5 suburbs or fewer, as randomForest() does for regression) on bootstrap samples of the 253 training suburbs and averages them. Each tree has low bias and high variance, and averaging cuts the variance. Checked against the R lab: bagging with mtry = 12 on the same split gives a test MSE of about 23.4, and randomForest() reports a "mean of squared residuals" of 11.40, which is its out-of-bag MSE; here bagging lands at about 23.5 and 11.3 with the default seed. The out-of-bag error sits well below the test error on this split. Both are valid estimates of the test error of the bagged model, but each is computed on its own half of the data, and this particular test half is harder to predict than the training half. The single tree is grown with the stopping rules of the pruning page (a node needs 10 suburbs, each child 5) and not pruned.

A random forest is bagging with one change: at every split only m of the 12 predictors, drawn at random, are candidates. With one strong predictor (rm) the bagged trees all start the same way and their predictions are highly correlated, so averaging them removes less variance than it would with independent trees (the ρσ² term never goes away). Forcing most splits to ignore rm decorrelates the trees. Each tree gets a little worse, but the average gets better. Checked against the R lab: with m = 6 the forest gives a test MSE of about 19.8, against the lab's 20.07. With m = 12 the forest is exactly bagging, and with m = 1 every split is on a random predictor, which is too much.

Boosting does not average independent trees: it grows small trees one after the other, each fit to the residuals of the model so far, and adds a shrunken copy of each. Here every tree is grown best-first until it has d splits (and each leaf keeps at least 5 suburbs). With λ = 0.2 and d = 4 the training MSE is practically zero after 500 trees while the test MSE stays near 16: the late trees fit noise, but each one is shrunk so much that the test error hardly suffers. With λ = 0.001 boosting needs thousands of trees (the R lab uses 5,000), so after 500 its curve is still far above the plot. The importance panel works like the relative influence that summary() reports for a gbm fit: the total drop in the RSS of the residuals from splits on each predictor, summed over the 500 trees (scaled here to the largest, not to 100).
