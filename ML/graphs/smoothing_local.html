The smoothing parameter and the span

ISLR Figures 7.8 and 7.9 on the Wage data (n = 3000 workers in the mid-Atlantic region, age 18 to 80, wage in thousands of dollars). Neither method chooses knots. A smoothing spline puts one at every distinct age and then shrinks the fit with a roughness penalty; local regression keeps no global fit at all and refits at every target point. Both have exactly one knob, and both read it off cross-validation.

  1. In Smoothing spline, drag λ from left to right and watch the effective degrees of freedom fall from 61 to 2, and the fit go from interpolation to the least squares line.
  2. Press Jump to the LOOCV λ. The third panel shows why that point is chosen, and the readout gives the effective degrees of freedom the course reports.
  3. Switch to Local regression and move the target age. Only the yellow points enter the fit, weighted by the bell, and the whole calculation is redone at every new target.

The two knobs do the same job through different machinery. The smoothing spline keeps one global fit with a knot at every distinct age, so its 61 nominal parameters are heavily constrained, and what is left free is measured by the effective degrees of freedom rather than counted. Pushing λ up trades variance for bias continuously, from the interpolating fit at one end to the least squares line at the other, and the LOOCV curve picks a point close to 7 on this data set. Local regression never forms a global fit: it keeps all the training data and solves a new weighted least squares problem at every target point, which is why it is called memory-based. Its span does the same work as λ, and a small span buys the same wiggliness a small λ does. What both curves show is that the penalty for being slightly too smooth is small on this data, while the penalty for being too rough is not.
