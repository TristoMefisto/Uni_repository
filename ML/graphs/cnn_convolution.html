Convolution and pooling, pixel by pixel

The first layers of a convolutional neural network on a 28 × 28 grayscale image, the size of the MNIST digits. A filter slides over the image and gives the convolved image, the ReLU of the detector layer keeps its positive part, and max pooling halves it. Draw your own image or pick one, edit the filter, and hover any pixel of the result to see the multiplications and the sum behind it. In a real CNN the filter values are parameters learned for the task; here you set them by hand.

  1. Pick a digit and the vertical edge filter. The convolved image lights up red on one side of every vertical stroke and blue on the other.
  2. Hover a pixel of the convolved image: the box at the bottom right shows the image patch under the filter, the products and their sum.
  3. Draw on the image (drag; right-drag or the eraser to rub out), shift it by one pixel, or type your own filter values and watch every layer update.

What a filter detects. Each pixel of the convolved image is a weighted sum of a small patch of the image, with the filter values as weights. It is large when the patch looks like the filter. The vertical edge filter has +1 in its left column and −1 in its right one, so it gives a large positive value where the image is dark on the left and light on the right, a large negative value for the opposite, and 0 on any flat patch (its weights sum to 0). The vertical strip filter is the 3 × 3 version of the slides' 15 × 15 filter with a narrow strip of ones, the one that picks out the tiger's vertical stripes. The same 9 numbers are used at every position (weight sharing), which is why a convolution layer has so few parameters compared with a fully connected one, and why a pattern is detected wherever it appears in the image.

From here to a CNN. A convolution layer uses a whole bank of filters, each giving one channel, and in a CNN their values are not chosen by hand: they are learned by gradient descent for the classification task, like the weights of any other layer. The ReLU keeps the positive matches, and pooling shrinks each channel and makes the result less sensitive to exactly where a feature sits. Deeper convolution layers then apply filters to these channels, so they detect combinations of edges (corners, loops, the eyes and ears of the tiger), and the last feature maps are flattened into an ordinary fully connected layer with a softmax output.
