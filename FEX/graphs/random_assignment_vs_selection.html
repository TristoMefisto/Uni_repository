Random assignment vs selection into treatment

Gerber & Green Table 2.1 again: one of the 7 villages gets a female head. If every village has the same chance (1/7), the difference in means is unbiased for the ATE of 5. Give some villages a higher chance and the same difference in means picks up two extra terms: selection on gains and baseline selection.

  1. Each village has a chance pi of being the treated one; by default all equal (1/7).
  2. Press One experiment: one village is drawn, its Y(1) is compared with the other six Y(0). Run 1 000 to see where the estimates centre.
  3. Switch to the in-class preset: the histogram centres on 5.74, not on the ATE of 5. The right panel splits the gap into its two parts.

Expected values are computed exactly from the probabilities: E[Y(1) | D=1] = Σᵢ pᵢ Y(1)ᵢ and E[Y(0) | D=0] = Σᵢ (1−pᵢ) Y(0)ᵢ / 6, where pᵢ is village i's chance of being treated. The Monte Carlo mean converges to the same number.

This is the critique of non-experimental comparisons: when "nature" assigns the treatment, the probabilities vary with the potential outcomes and nobody knows them. With known unequal probabilities, weighting each observation by the inverse of its assignment probability recovers the ATE.
