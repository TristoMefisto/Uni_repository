Noncompliance, the ITT and the LATE

A population made of the 4 latent types of L6 (compliers, always-takers, never-takers and defiers), each with its own share and its own average treatment effect. Assignment Z is random. The page computes what an experiment would actually estimate (the two intention-to-treat effects and their ratio) and compares it with the LATE, the effect among compliers. All the numbers are population expectations, so there is no sampling noise.

  1. Load Table 5.1: one-sided noncompliance, and the ratio of the two ITTs is exactly the LATE.
  2. Load Table 6.1, or add defiers by hand. The ratio stops being the LATE, because defiers pull both ITTs in the wrong direction.
  3. Give assignment a small direct effect on the outcome and lower the share of compliers: the bias explodes as ITTD goes to zero.

The defaults reproduce Table 5.1: two-thirds compliers with an average effect of 3, one-third never-takers with an average effect of 6. ITTD = 2/3, ITTY = 2 and the ratio is 3, the LATE, while the ATE is 4 and cannot be recovered from the data. Table 6.1 has 4 compliers (effect 10), 2 defiers (effect 5), 1 always-taker and 3 never-takers out of 10: ITTY = 3, ITTD = 0.2 and the ratio is 15 instead of 10. Imposing monotonicity removes the defiers and the ratio goes back to the LATE.

The direct effect of Z stands for a violation of the exclusion restriction (the encouragement itself changes the outcome). It enters ITTY one for one, and the ratio divides it by ITTD, so a direct effect of 0.5 with 5% compliers adds 10 to the estimate. This is the point of the discussion slide: when the rate of noncompliance is very high, even a slight violation of the exclusion restriction can severely bias the LATE.
