The regression framework behind the steps

The model is three linked equations. The policy effect is the product of their slopes: people who stop smoking, times the CVD risk attached to smoking, times the cost attached to CVD.

1. Smoking calibrated

π₁ = π₀ · (P₁ / P₀)ε
  • π is smoking prevalence, P the pack price, ε the participation elasticity.
  • Cigarette prices are the same everywhere in Italy, so one survey cannot estimate ε. It is taken from studies that use price changes over time.
  • In a logit for smoking this is the same as adding ε ÷ (1 − π₀) × ln(P₁ / P₀) to each person's log-odds.

2. Disease estimated and benchmarked

Pr(CVD) = Λ(α + β·Smoker + X′γ)
  • Logit on EHIS 2019, 43,759 adults; controls for age class × sex, education and region.
  • Ever-smokers have 23% higher odds of CVD (odds ratio 1.23).
  • Converted to a probability difference, δ = 1.29 percentage points; a linear probability model gives 1.19 (standard error 0.27).
  • Benchmark from cohort studies: Pr(CVD) = P₀·[1 + π(RR − 1)], so δ = P₀·(RR − 1), computed by age class.

3. Cost estimated and benchmarked

Cost = c₀ + θ·CVD + X′γ
  • θ is the extra yearly cost of a person with CVD.
  • On EHIS 2019, a person with CVD has 1.01 more hospital nights a year, 0.45 more GP visits and 0.27 more specialist visits per four weeks.
  • In euros, θ is the national cost of CVD divided by the number of people with CVD.

When the gains arrive

Smoking falls within a year or two of a price rise. The health gain follows slowly, because a former smoker's extra risk fades over many years. The figures above are the long-run result.

Limitations of the model

These are steady-state estimates built from survey associations and published parameters. They show the order of magnitude of the effect, not a forecast.

Inputs

  • The elasticity is borrowed, not estimated. Cigarette prices do not vary across Italy, so it comes from the literature. The result is almost proportional to it.
  • Sources do not line up exactly. Smoking prevalence, population and CVD prevalence come from surveys with different years and age ranges, and all are self-reported.
  • Full pass-through is assumed. Producers may absorb part of a tax on cheap brands or add to it on premium ones. Part of Italy's excise is proportional to price, so raising only the fixed component by €1 would lift prices by more than shown.

How smokers respond

  • No leakage. Cross-border purchases, illicit cigarettes and switching to roll-your-own or heated tobacco all reduce the real price rise.
  • Only quitting counts. Smokers who cut down are left out; their CVD risk falls only a little.
  • Large increases are extrapolation. The same elasticity is applied at every tax level. Beyond the price changes Italy has experienced, the curve is a guess.
  • Who quits matters. Price-sensitive smokers tend to be younger, with low CVD risk today. The "only younger smokers quit" option shows how much this lowers the result.

The disease step

  • Long run, not year one. Quitters keep part of their extra risk for 10 to 25 years.
  • One relative risk for everyone. It is applied to all ages and both sexes, and former smokers are treated as non-smokers.
  • The survey regression is an association. People quit after a diagnosis and some smokers with CVD have died, which pulls the estimate down. It is used as a lower bound. It compares people who ever smoked with those who never did, so applying it to quitters assumes their risk fully reverts.
  • Narrow definition of CVD. Four self-reported conditions; high blood pressure is not included.

Costs, deaths and what is left out

  • Average cost, not marginal cost. Hospitals' fixed costs do not fall in proportion to patients. The cost study also defines CVD more broadly than the survey, so cost per case is overstated.
  • Savings are gross. They ignore health costs in the extra years of life, and the tax revenue itself, which is a transfer.
  • Deaths are indicative. They are scaled in proportion to cases, but CVD deaths occur mostly at ages where few people smoke.
  • Benefits not counted. Cancer and lung disease, second-hand smoke, young people who never start, and the effect of an anti-smoking campaign funded by the revenue.
