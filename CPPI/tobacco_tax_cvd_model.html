Raise the cigarette excise: fewer smokers, fewer CVD admissions and deaths

A higher specific excise raises the price of a pack. Following the WHO assumption (Goodchild et al., 2016), half of smokers' response comes from people who quit and half from people who keep smoking but smoke less. Only quitting lowers the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), so the health benefits come from the fall in smokers, while both responses lower the packs sold. How the numbers are built

Five steps, the same as in the appendix of the brief (Table A2), each with its formula, its inputs and its result for the excise you chose. All inputs are those of Table A1; only the excise moves.

1

From the excise to the pack price Table A2, steps 1–2

We assume that the whole rise is passed on to smokers. The ad valorem excise, VAT and the retailer's margin are shares of the price, while the manufacturer keeps the same euros per pack (€0.59). So each euro of specific excise raises the price by about €4.40, and the State gets 90% of the price rise (72% as excise and 18% as VAT).

ΔP = ΔS / (1 − a − v − r), ΔP% = ΔP / P₀
E = S + aP, T = E + vP

From the price to smokers Table A2, steps 3–4

Goodchild et al. (2016) assume that in high-income countries a 1% rise in price cuts cigarette consumption by 0.3% within one to three years. Half of the cut comes from people who quit and half from people who keep smoking but smoke less. Each change is the elasticity times the price rise (the linear form of their eq. 3).

εprev = φ εtot, εint = (1 − φ) εtot
Ns¹ = Ns (1 + εprev ΔP%)
  • εtottotal price elasticity, −0.30 (range −0.25 to −0.50)
  • φshare of the response from quitting, 50%, so εprev = εint = −0.15
  • Ns, Pesmokers today, 9,999,000 (18.6% of people 11+)

3

CVD admissions, deaths and costs avoided Table A2, step 5; Table A4

Only quitting lowers the risk of CVD (smoking less does not, Jeong et al., 2021). We take the part of the CVD burden caused by tobacco (18.8%) and reduce it in proportion to the fall in smokers. Quitters do not lose all their extra risk at once: we assume they remove 31% of it, the value for people who quit less than five years before (Mons et al., 2015), so the figures are for the medium run.

avoided = B × s_tob × (−εprev ΔP%) × ρ
  • BCVD burden in Italy: costs and admissions in 2021, deaths in 2017 (table below)
  • s_tobshare of the CVD burden due to tobacco, 18.8% (of DALYs, 2017)
  • ρshare of the extra risk removed by quitting, 31%: CVD mortality risk 2.07 for current smokers and 1.74 for people who quit less than 5 years before, against never smokers

4

Fewer cigarettes per continuing smoker, by type of smoker Table A2, steps 6–7; Table A5

Smokers who keep smoking cut their cigarettes by εint ΔP% on average. Crespi et al. (2021) split Italian smokers into five types that react very differently to the price. We multiply all their elasticities by one factor k, so that their mean weighted by cigarettes equals Goodchild's −0.15. The split changes no total: it shows who cuts and whose excise is not collected. Cutting down brings no CVD benefit, so this step only moves packs and revenue.

q¹ = q⁰ (1 + εint ΔP%)
w_q = s_q Q_q / Σ s_j Q_j, k = εint / Σ w_q η_q, η*_q = k η_q

5

Packs sold and tax revenue Table A2, steps 8–9; Table A6

Packs are smokers times cigarettes per smoker, so the two responses multiply. Revenue is packs times the tax on each pack. Packs fall by less than the tax rises, so revenue goes up. Revenue is a transfer from smokers to the State, not a saving, so it is kept apart from the health benefits (excise is the main figure, VAT is shown apart).

Limitations

The figures are a calibration from published parameters (orders of magnitude, and which input drives the result), so they are not a forecast. Smokers' and producers' welfare is not counted, so this is not a cost-benefit analysis.

What is left out (so the benefit is understated)

  • Diseases other than CVD (cancers, COPD) and second-hand smoke
  • Young people who do not start smoking: only today's smokers are counted
  • Gains after the medium run: quitters' risk keeps falling after the first years
  • The non-fiscal measures (plain packaging, outdoor smoking bans), which the brief discusses only in words

What may overstate it

  • The 18.8% is a share of DALYs, and it also covers former smokers and second-hand smoke; it is applied to costs, admissions and deaths alike
  • Smokers who quit because of the price tend to be light smokers
  • Smokers who move to roll-your-own, heated tobacco or e-cigarettes count as quitters (the brief raises roll-your-own and heated tobacco by the same 21.5% to prevent this)
  • Savings are gross: quitters live longer and use other services later

Parameters

  • One short-run elasticity, applied linearly to a 21% rise (a constant-elasticity form gives effects about 10% smaller)
  • The 50% split is a rounding of US data from 1976-1993
  • The 31% is a ratio of CVD deaths among people aged 60+ (mostly from the US), applied to admissions and costs too
  • Crespi's types come from a 2016 survey of 948 smokers, so the split by type is an illustration
  • The data refer to different years: 2017 (deaths and the tobacco share), 2021 (costs and admissions), 2024 (packs), 2025 (smokers)

Policy

  • The minimum tax (€216 per 1,000) must rise with the specific excise (to about €266), or cheap brands escape the rise; at €5.40 the average pack sits just below it, so the extra excise may be up to €140m lower
  • Indexation to inflation is proposed but not in the numbers: without it, inflation slowly erodes the rise
  • The target is the unweighted mean of the 27 EU prices, not adjusted for purchasing power
  • The tax is regressive: it weighs more on poorer smokers
